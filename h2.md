### a) Podcast – Darknet Diaries, Episode 111: Zeus

- ZeuS is a banking Trojan designed to steal money from online banking users accounts. It eventually became one of the most widespread banking malware operations and led to a major international law enforcement investigation.
- It was created by a young 22 year old Russian hacker who used several online aliases but eventually became known as Slavik.
- The malware first appeared in 2006 under the name WSNPoem. By December 2007, hackers had stolen over 200k $ from commercial bank accounts across the US, UK, Italy and Spain.
- ZeuS spread through spam emails and drive-by downloads. Once installed, it searched the infected computer for stored credentials. When users logged into their online bank accounts, the malware alerted the hackers, who could take control of the banking session and transfer money.
- After its early success, Slavik continued developing the malware and released a new version called Zbot, short for ZeuS Bot. It not only stole banking credentials but also infected computers and made them part of a botnet, a large network of infected machines controlled by hackers.
- Slavik built ZeuS as a DIY hacker toolkit that could be sold to other criminals. This allowed hackers to buy the malware and create their own botnets without developing everything themselves. Many criminal groups started using it to steal money from online banking accounts.
- By May 2009, the FBI was receiving reports of large fraudulent bank transfers without any obvious evidence of a security breach. The transactions appeared to come directly from the customers' own computers, browsers and IP addresses, which initially made investigators question whether the customers were committing fraud themselves.
- In 2009, Slavik teamed up with a hacker group called Avalanche and developed a more advanced version called JabberZeuS. It included additional modules that could be purchased separately, such as features that notified hackers when an infected user logged into a bank account containing a certain amount of money.
- JabberZeuS could also capture two-factor authentication codes, allowing criminals to bypass additional banking security measures. This helped the group target corporate accounts containing hundreds of thousands of dollars.
- To move the stolen money, the criminals recruited money mules through fake job advertisements on websites such as Craigslist. Victims were offered ordinary remote jobs and asked to receive money into their bank accounts and forward it elsewhere, keeping around 5% as payment. Many did not realize they were participating in money laundering.
- One major attack targeted Bullitt County Fiscal Court in Kentucky in June 2009. The hackers compromised the county treasurer's computer, created 25 fake employees in the payroll system and transferred approximately 415k $ to money mules. The bank detected suspicious activity and some of the money was recovered.
- In September 2009, the FBI managed to trace a server used by the JabberZeuS group. Investigators discovered server logs, stolen banking credentials, victim information and private chat conversations between the hackers. This provided valuable evidence for the investigation.
- ZeuS eventually faced competition from another banking Trojan called SpyEye. Its creators tried to attract ZeuS customers with a cheaper product. The two malware programs were even designed to detect and remove each other from infected computers.
- In 2011, the complete ZeuS source code was leaked online, allowing other criminals to develop their own versions. Slavik also introduced a Malware-as-a-Service (MaaS) business model, where customers could access the malware through a subscription-based service.
- A later version called Gameover ZeuS became even more advanced. It used peer-to-peer technology, making the botnet harder to shut down because it did not rely on a single central command server. The criminals also started distributing CryptoLocker ransomware through the botnet, encrypting victims' files and demanding ransom payments.
- In 2014, the FBI identified Slavik as Evgeniy Bogachev, a Russian national. He was charged with several cybercrime offences, including bank fraud and money laundering.
- In the same year, the FBI and international law enforcement agencies, together with cybersecurity companies, launched a coordinated operation to disrupt Gameover ZeuS and CryptoLocker. They seized servers and redirected infected computers away from the criminals' infrastructure.
- Despite the operation, Bogachev was never arrested. He was placed on the FBI's Cyber's Most Wanted list, with a 3 million dollars reward for information leading to his arrest.
- ZeuS is estimated to have infected between 500,000 and one million computers worldwide. According to the FBI, US victims alone lost over 100 million dollars through fraudulent bank transfers, with an additional 27 million dollars collected through ransomware payments.

### b) Blog - Traficom: Yellow alert: Data breaches in Microsoft 365 accounts as a result of phishing

- The article describes a phishing campaign targeting Microsoft 365 accounts in Finnish organizations.
- Criminals sent fake emails that looked like legitimate secure email notifications. The messages contained links leading to phishing websites designed to steal users login credentials.
- Campaign was particularly effective because the messages looked convincing and used familiar communication methods. Dozens of Finnish organizations reported phishing attempts and compromised accounts.
- Once attackers obtain a user's username and password, they could access their Microsoft 365 account and potentially use it to send further phishing messages to the user's contacts.
- This allowed the attacks to spread between organizations, as messages sent from compromised accounts appeared more trustworthy to recipients.
- The National Cyber Security Centre Finland recommended using forced multi-factor authentication (MFA) to prevent unauthorized access, even if passwords had been stolen.
- However, MFA alone is not enough. Organizations should also train employees to recognize phishing attempts and pay attention to the websites where they enter their credentials.
- Main lesson is that attackers do not always need to exploit technical vulnerabilities. They can also gain access by manipulating users into revealing their login credentials, for example through fake emails, phishing websites or messages that appear to come from trusted organizations.

### c) Science that – ZeuS: Applying Threat Modeling to a Real-World Cyberattack

- In class, we studied threat modeling and how to evaluate risks based on the attackers capabilities, opportunities and intentions (COI).
- The ZeuS banking trojan is a good example of how these three factors come together in a real cyberattack:
  - Capability: The attackers had the technical skills and malware needed to infect computers, steal banking credentials and bypass security measures.
  - Opportunity: They took advantage of online banking systems and vulnerable user devices to gain access to banking sessions and transfer money.
  - Intent: Their main goal was financial gain by stealing money from individuals and companies.
- The impact was significant, as the attackers managed to steal millions of dollars through fraudulent bank transfers.
- We can also use the META approach to think about how organizations could reduce these risks. For example, banks could improve transaction verification, monitor suspicious payments and strengthen endpoint security.
- This shows how threat modeling can help organizations understand how attacks happen, identify weaknesses and decide how to protect themselves.
